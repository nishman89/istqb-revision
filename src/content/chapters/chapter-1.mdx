## 1.1 What is Testing?

### What is Testing?

<SyllabusRef ref="1.1" />

**Software testing** is a set of activities to discover **defects** and evaluate the **quality** of software artifacts — known as **test objects** while being tested.

<MythReality rows={[
  { myth: 'Testing is just executing tests', reality: 'Testing includes many other activities — planning, analysis, design, reporting… — aligned with the SDLC (Chapter 2)' },
  { myth: 'Testing only verifies the test object (does it meet the specified requirements?)', reality: 'Testing also involves validation — checking it meets users\' and stakeholders\' needs in its operational environment' },
]} />

<SyllabusText>

Software testing is a set of activities to discover defects and evaluate the quality of software artifacts. These artifacts, when being tested, are known as test objects.

Misconception: testing only consists of executing tests. Testing includes many other activities that must be aligned with the SDLC (Chapter 2).

Misconception: testing focuses entirely on verifying the test object (checking whether the system meets the specified requirements).

Actually: testing also involves validation — checking whether the system meets users' and other stakeholders' needs in its operational environment.

</SyllabusText>

### When Software Fails

<SyllabusRef ref="1.1" />

Testing assesses software quality and helps reduce the risk of software failure in operation. When software does fail,
the consequences can be catastrophic: loss of money, time and business reputation — even injury or death.

<Exercise kind="discuss" title="Think about it" id="discuss-when-software-fails">

When have you experienced software that did not work as expected? What were the consequences?

</Exercise>

#### Case study 1: Horizon and the UK Post Office scandal

The Post Office's accounting software, **Horizon**, had faults that made branch accounts show money that was missing
when it wasn't. The Post Office trusted the system over its staff, and **hundreds of subpostmasters were falsely accused
of theft, fraud and false accounting**. Many were prosecuted, made to repay "shortfalls" from their own savings,
or lost their businesses — ruining careers and lives. Convictions were later overturned once the system's defects
came to light. [Read more on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Post_Office_scandal).

- **What failed** - accounting software reporting shortfalls that didn't exist
- **The lesson** - software output is not proof; failures in operation can do enormous human harm

#### Case study 2: Boeing 737 MAX and MCAS

The **Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS)** was designed to push the aircraft's nose down in certain conditions.
A failure in MCAS contributed to **two fatal crashes in which 346 people died**. The aircraft was **grounded for 20 months**,
and Boeing faced around **$20 billion** in fines and legal fees.

- **What failed** - flight-control software acting on bad data, which pilots struggled to override
- **The lesson** - in safety-critical systems, failures can cost lives — so the testing must be rigorous and independent

### Verification vs Validation

Testing checks a product in two different ways. Both are part of testing, and the exam expects you to tell them apart.

### More Than Running Tests

<SyllabusRef ref="1.1" />

Testing is **much more than running tests**. It is a whole set of activities that runs alongside development, and it
should be **aligned with the software development lifecycle (SDLC)** you're using. Chapter 2 covers this in detail.

- **Static testing** - Reviews and static analysis · The software is not run
- **Dynamic testing** - The software is run · Test techniques are used to design the test cases
- **Planned and managed** - Like any project work, testing is estimated, monitored and controlled
- **Supported by tools, led by people** - Tools help, but testing relies on specialist knowledge, analytical skills, critical thinking and systems thinking

<Resource kind="standard" href="https://www.iso.org/standard/81291.html" title="ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119 — the international software testing standard" text="The standard behind many of the test process terms used in the syllabus" />

<SyllabusText>

Testing includes many activities and must be aligned with the software development lifecycle (see chapter 2).

Testing may be dynamic or static. Dynamic testing involves the execution of software, while static testing does not.

Static testing includes reviews and static analysis (see chapter 3).

Dynamic testing uses different types of test techniques and test approaches to derive test cases (see chapter 4).

Testing is not only a technical activity. It also needs to be properly planned, managed, estimated, monitored and controlled (see chapter 5).

Testers use tools (see chapter 6), but it is important to remember that testing is largely an intellectual activity, requiring the testers to have specialized knowledge, use analytical skills and apply critical thinking and systems thinking (Myers 2011, Roman 2018).

The ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-1 standard provides further information about software testing concepts.

</SyllabusText>

### Testing vs Debugging

<SyllabusRef ref="1.1.2" />

<DebuggingFlow />

<div class="callout callout--remember">

**❗ Remember:** **Testing finds** defects — it's mainly done by **testers** (though anyone can test).
**Debugging fixes** them — it's done by **developers**. Defects themselves can be introduced by *anyone* who makes an
error: analysts, developers, even testers. After the fix, the **tester** runs confirmation testing — ideally the same
person who ran the original test.

</div>

- **Debugging is not testing** - It's a development activity: finding, analysing and removing the cause of a failure
- **Static testing finds the defect directly** - Nothing is run, so there's no failure to reproduce or diagnose — only the fix is needed
- **Confirmation testing** - Ideally run by the person who ran the original test

<SyllabusText>

Dynamic testing can trigger failures that are caused by defects in the software.

When dynamic testing triggers a failure, debugging is concerned with: reproduction of the failure, diagnosis (finding the root cause), and fixing the cause.

Subsequent confirmation testing checks whether the fixes resolved the problem. Regression testing checks whether the fixes are causing failures in other parts of the test object.

Static testing directly finds defects, which should then be fixed. There is no need for reproduction or diagnosis, since static testing cannot trigger failures.

</SyllabusText>

## 1.2 Why is Testing Necessary?

### Why is Testing Necessary?

<SyllabusRef ref="1.2" />

Software is built by people, and people make mistakes. Every project also has agreed goals for **scope, time,
quality and budget**. Testing is how a project finds those mistakes early and checks it is on course to meet its goals.

- **A form of quality control** - Helps the project hit its agreed goals for scope, time, quality and budget
- **Not just the test team's job** - Any stakeholder can use testing skills to move the project closer to success
- **Finds defects** - Testing components, systems and their documentation helps identify defects

<SyllabusText>

Testing, as a form of quality control, helps in achieving the agreed upon goals within the set scope, time, quality, and budget constraints.

Testing's contribution to success should not be restricted to the test team activities. Any stakeholder can use their testing skills to bring the project closer to success.

Testing components, systems, and associated documentation helps to identify defects in software.

</SyllabusText>

### Testing's Contribution to Success

<SyllabusRef ref="1.2.1" />

Testing doesn't just find bugs. It contributes to a project's success in four main ways:

- **Cost-effective defect detection** - Defects are then removed by debugging — indirectly raising quality
- **Evaluates quality at each stage** - Measurements feed decisions such as moving to the next stage or releasing
- **Represents the users** - Makes sure their needs are considered throughout the lifecycle
- **Meets obligations** - Contractual or legal requirements, and regulatory standards

<SyllabusText>

Testing:

Provides a cost-effective means of detecting defects, which can be removed by debugging (a non-testing activity), indirectly contributing to higher quality test objects.

Provides a means of directly evaluating the quality of a test object at various stages in the SDLC. These measurements are used as part of a larger project management activity, contributing to decisions to move to the next stage of the SDLC, such as the release decision.

Indirectly represents the users on the development project, which ensures their needs are considered throughout the development lifecycle.

May be required to meet contractual or legal requirements, or to comply with regulatory standards.

</SyllabusText>

### Testing and Quality Assurance

<SyllabusRef ref="1.2.2" />

Testing and quality assurance (QA) are often confused. They are related, but they are not the same thing:

<Compare caption="Testing is a form of quality control; quality assurance improves the process (CTFL 1.2.2)" columns={[
  { title: 'Quality control', sub: 'product-oriented · corrective', points: ['Finds and fixes defects in the product', 'Testing, formal methods, simulation, prototyping'] },
  { title: 'Quality assurance', sub: 'process-oriented · preventive', points: ['Implements and improves processes', 'A good process gives a good product', "Everyone's responsibility"] },
]} />

- **Related, but not the same** - Testing is a form of quality control; QA is about the processes
- **Test results feed QA** - They show how well the development and test processes are working, so QA can improve them

<SyllabusText>

Testing and Quality Assurance (QA) are not the same. Testing is a form of Quality Control (QC).

QC is a product-oriented, corrective approach that focuses on those activities supporting the achievement of appropriate levels of quality. Examples include testing, formal methods (model checking and proof of correctness), simulation and prototyping.

QA is a process-oriented, preventive approach that focuses on the implementation and improvement of processes. It works on the basis that if a good process is followed correctly, then it will generate a good product. QA applies to both the development and testing processes, and is the responsibility of everyone on a project.

</SyllabusText>

### Errors, Defects and Failures

<SyllabusRef ref="1.2.3" />

<DefectChain />

- **Error** - Also called a *mistake* · A human action that produces an incorrect result
- **Defect** - Also called a *fault* or *bug* · An imperfection in a work product — documentation, code or a build file
- **Failure** - The system does not do what it should, or does something it shouldn't, when it runs

> **Not every defect causes a failure.** Failures can also come from environmental conditions,
> e.g. radiation or electromagnetic fields damaging firmware.

<SyllabusText>

Human beings make errors (mistakes), which produce defects (faults, bugs), which in turn may result in failures.

Humans make errors for various reasons, such as time pressure, complexity of work products, processes, infrastructure or interactions, or simply because they are tired or lack adequate training.

Defects can be found in documentation, such as a requirements specification or a test script, in source code, or in a supporting artifact such as a build file. Defects in artifacts produced earlier in the SDLC, if undetected, often lead to defective artifacts later in the lifecycle.

If a defect in code is executed, the system may fail to do what it should do, or do something it shouldn't, causing a failure. Some defects will always result in a failure if executed, while others will only result in a failure in specific circumstances, and some may never result in a failure.

Errors and defects are not the only cause of failures. Failures can also be caused by environmental conditions, such as when radiation or electromagnetic fields cause defects in firmware.

</SyllabusText>

### Find It Early, Fix It Once

<SyllabusRef ref="1.2.3" />

When a failure or defect is found, the aim is not only to fix it, but to stop the same kind of problem happening again:

- Identify and fix the defect whenever a failure or defect is found
- **Understand** - Find what error was made, and what caused it
- **Prevent** - Stop further errors, defects and failures with the same cause
- Earlier in the lifecycle
- **The earlier we do this, the better** - fewer derived work products to rework, and less effort to fix.

<SyllabusText>

Whenever a failure occurs or a defect is identified, we need to identify and fix the defect.

If we can identify why the defect occurred (what error was made, and what was its cause), we can prevent further errors, defects and failures.

The earlier we do this in the development process, the better.

</SyllabusText>

## 1.3 The Seven Testing Principles

### The Seven Testing Principles

<SyllabusRef ref="1.3" />

<Tiles caption="The seven testing principles (CTFL 1.3)" tiles={[

  { label: '3', title: 'Early testing saves time and money' }, { label: '4', title: 'Defects cluster together', sub: 'the Pareto principle' },
  { label: '5', title: 'Tests wear out', sub: 'the pesticide paradox' }, { label: '6', title: 'Testing is context dependent' },

### Principle 1: Testing shows the presence, not the absence, of defects

<SyllabusRef ref="1.3" />

> **In a nutshell:** Testing can show that defects are present, but cannot prove there are none. It reduces the chance of undiscovered defects remaining.

<Exercise kind="discuss" title="Think about it" id="discuss-principle-1-testing-shows-the-presence-not-the-absence-of-defects">

If no defects are found, does that mean the software is correct?

<div slot="answer">

No. Testing can show that defects are present, but it can't prove there are none. If you didn't find any, it may mean the tests weren't good enough — you never know how many defects remain unfound.

</div>
</Exercise>

<SyllabusText>

Testing can show that defects are present in the test object, but cannot prove that there are no defects (Buxton 1970). Testing reduces the probability of defects remaining undiscovered in the test object, but even if no defects are found, testing cannot prove test object correctness.

</SyllabusText>

### Principle 2: Exhaustive testing is impossible

<SyllabusRef ref="1.3" />

> **In a nutshell:** Testing everything isn't feasible except in trivial cases. Use test techniques, prioritisation and risk-based testing to focus effort.

<Exercise kind="discuss" title="Think about it" id="discuss-principle-2-exhaustive-testing-is-impossible">

How many tests would a "Good morning / Good afternoon" greeting need?

<div slot="answer">

At least two — one before noon and one after. But do you test every second between 00:00:01 and 23:59:59? Fractions of seconds? Different time formats? The switch to and from daylight saving time? Testing everything is impossible, so we use techniques, priorities and risk to choose.

</div>
</Exercise>

<SyllabusText>

Testing everything is not feasible except in trivial cases (Manna 1978). Rather than attempting to test exhaustively, test techniques (see chapter 4), test case prioritization (see section 5.1.5), and risk-based testing (see section 5.2) should be used to focus test efforts.

</SyllabusText>

### Principle 3: Early testing saves time and money

<SyllabusRef ref="1.3" />

> **In a nutshell:** Defects removed early don't cause further defects in derived work products, so fewer failures occur later in the SDLC.

<Exercise kind="discuss" title="Think about it" id="discuss-principle-3-early-testing-saves-time-and-money">

On a new project, what could we test before any code exists?

<div slot="answer">

Plenty: review the requirements and user stories, check the acceptance criteria are clear and testable, review designs, and start test analysis and design. Defects found here are the cheapest to fix.

</div>
</Exercise>

<SyllabusText>

Defects that are removed early in the process will not cause subsequent defects in derived work products. The cost of quality will be reduced since fewer failures will occur later in the SDLC (Boehm 1981). To find defects early, both static testing (see chapter 3) and dynamic testing (see chapter 4) should be started as early as possible.

</SyllabusText>

### Principle 4: Defects cluster together

<SyllabusRef ref="1.3" />

> **In a nutshell:** A small number of components usually contain most of the defects or cause most failures — an example of the Pareto principle.

<Exercise kind="discuss" title="Think about it" id="discuss-principle-4-defects-cluster-together">

Why do you think defects cluster together?

<div slot="answer">

Often they share a root cause — less experienced developers, rushed code, too little unit testing, or an unfamiliar library or design. A few early defects can also have knock-on effects. It's an observation (the Pareto principle), not a law.

</div>
</Exercise>

<SyllabusText>

A small number of system components usually contain most of the defects discovered or are responsible for most of the operational failures (Enders 1975). This phenomenon is an illustration of the Pareto principle. Predicted defect clusters, and actual defect clusters observed during testing or in operation, are an important input for risk-based testing (see section 5.2).

</SyllabusText>

### Principle 5: Tests wear out

<SyllabusRef ref="1.3" />

> **In a nutshell:** Repeating the same tests makes them less effective at finding new defects — also known as the pesticide paradox.

<Exercise kind="discuss" title="Think about it" id="discuss-principle-5-tests-wear-out">

Why does this happen? And when is repeating tests actually useful?

<div slot="answer">

The defects those tests can find have already been found and fixed — like spraying pesticide on bugs that are already dead. To find new defects, change the tests and data, or test less-explored areas. Repeating tests is still valuable as automated regression testing, to catch new defects as soon as they're introduced.

</div>
</Exercise>

<SyllabusText>

If the same tests are repeated many times, they become increasingly ineffective in detecting new defects (Beizer 1990). To overcome this effect, existing tests and test data may need to be modified, and new tests may need to be written. However, in some cases, repeating the same tests can have a beneficial outcome, e.g., in automated regression testing (see section 2.2.3).

</SyllabusText>

### Principle 6: Testing is context dependent

<SyllabusRef ref="1.3" />

> **In a nutshell:** There is no single universally applicable approach to testing — it is done differently in different contexts.

<Exercise kind="discuss" title="Think about it" id="discuss-principle-6-testing-is-context-dependent">

How would testing a games console differ from testing a space shuttle?

<div slot="answer">

The risk is completely different. A space shuttle is safety- and mission-critical, so it needs formal, highly rigorous testing, independence and documentation. A games console needs strong usability and performance testing, with more emphasis on time to market.

</div>
</Exercise>

<SyllabusText>

There is no single universally applicable approach to testing. Testing is done differently in different contexts (Kaner 2011).

</SyllabusText>

### Principle 7: Absence-of-defects fallacy

<SyllabusRef ref="1.3" />

> **In a nutshell:** Fixing every defect against the specified requirements can still give a system that doesn't meet users' needs. Verification alone isn't enough — validate too.

<Exercise kind="discuss" title="Think about it" id="discuss-principle-7-absence-of-defects-fallacy">

What do you think this means?

<div slot="answer">

Finding and fixing lots of defects doesn't guarantee success. A system can meet every specified requirement and still not meet the users' needs, not help the business, or be worse than competitors. That's why we validate as well as verify.

</div>
</Exercise>

<SyllabusText>

It is a fallacy (i.e., a misconception) to expect that software verification will ensure the success of a system. Thoroughly testing all the specified requirements and fixing all the defects found could still produce a system that does not fulfill the users' needs and expectations, that does not help in achieving the customer's business goals, and that is inferior compared to other competing systems. In addition to verification, validation should also be carried out (Boehm 1981).

</SyllabusText>

## 1.4 Test Activities, Testware and Test Roles

### The Test Process

<SyllabusRef ref="1.4.1" />

<TestProcess />

- **Test Planning** - Define test objectives and select an approach
- **Test Analysis** - Identify WHAT to test
- **Test Design** - Decide in detail HOW to test
- **Test Implementation** - Set up the test environment
- **Test Execution** - Run and report on the tests
- **Test Completion** - Finalise and hand over at the end
- **Test Monitoring & Control** - Keep the project on track — runs throughout
- These activities are often iterative or run in parallel — not a strict sequence.

### Test Activities — Key Tasks

<SyllabusRef ref="1.4.1" />

Each activity in the test process is made up of a few key tasks. This table shows what testers actually do in each one:

| Activity | Key tasks |
|---|---|
| Test Planning | Define test objectives · Select approach |
| Monitoring & Control | Check test activities · Compare actual progress against plan · Take action where necessary |
| Test Analysis | Identify testable features · Define & prioritise test conditions · Analyse risks |
| Test Design | Create test cases and other testware · Identify coverage items (a guide to test inputs) · Define test data requirements, design the test environment, identify infrastructure & tools |
| Test Implementation | Acquire or create test data · Create test procedures, suites, scripts and prioritised schedules · Set up test environment |
| Test Execution | Run tests and log results · Analyse anomalies to identify likely causes · Create reports |
| Test Completion | Resolve loose ends · Archive testware · Shut down test environment · Write and distribute the test completion report |

### Test Process in Context

<SyllabusRef ref="1.4.2" />

Testing isn't done in isolation. It's part of the organisation's development process and is funded by stakeholders,
so **how we test depends on the context**:

- **Stakeholders** - needs, expectations, requirements, willingness to cooperate
- **Team members** - skills, knowledge, level of experience, availability, training needs
- **Business domain** - criticality of the test object, identified risks, market needs, legal regulations
- **Technical factors** - type of software, product architecture, technology used
- **Project constraints** - scope, time, budget, resources
- **Organisational factors** - organisational structure, existing policies, practices used
- **SDLC** - engineering practices, development methods
- **Tools** - availability, usability, compliance

#### These factors shape

- Test strategy
- Test techniques
- Degree of automation
- Level of coverage
- Documentation detail
- Reporting

### Testware

<SyllabusRef ref="1.4.3" />

**Testware** is everything that testing produces: plans, test cases, test scripts, test data, logs, reports and so on.
Each activity in the test process creates its own testware, as this table shows:

| Activity | Testware produced |
|---|---|
| Test Planning | Test plan, test schedule, risk register, entry & exit criteria |

### Traceability

<SyllabusRef ref="1.4.4" />

Traceability means keeping links between the test basis (such as requirements) and everything testing produces, so you can follow any item forwards or backwards:

- Test basis
- Testware

- **Enables monitoring & control** - Maintain links across the whole test process

<SyllabusText>

In order to implement effective test monitoring and control, it is important to establish and maintain traceability throughout the test process between the test basis elements, testware associated with these elements, test results, and detected defects.

Accurate traceability supports coverage evaluation, so it is very useful if measurable coverage criteria are defined in the test basis.

</SyllabusText>

### Why Traceability Matters

<SyllabusRef ref="1.4.4" />

- **Requirements coverage** - Test cases ↔ requirements shows requirements are covered
- **Residual risk** - Test results ↔ risks shows how much risk remains
- **Impact of change** - See what is affected when something changes
- **Audits & governance** - Facilitates test audits and IT governance criteria
- **Clearer reporting** - Status of test basis elements makes reports understandable
- **Progress vs goals** - Assess quality, process capability and project progress

<SyllabusText>

Traceability of test cases to requirements can verify that the requirements are covered by test cases.

Traceability of test results to risks can be used to evaluate the level of residual risk in a test object.

In addition to evaluating coverage, good traceability makes it possible to determine the impact of changes, facilitates test audits, and helps meet IT governance criteria.

Good traceability also makes test progress and completion reports more easily understandable by including the status of test basis elements. This can also assist in communicating the technical aspects of testing to stakeholders in an understandable manner.

Traceability provides information to assess product quality, process capability, and project progress against business goals.

</SyllabusText>

### Key Terms

### Communication Skills

<SyllabusRef ref="1.5.1" />

#### The challenge

- **Testers bring bad news** - …and people tend to blame the messenger
- **Results feel like criticism** - of the product and of its author
- **Confirmation bias** - makes conflicting information hard to accept
- **"Testing is destructive"** - despite its big contribution to project success

> **The response:** communicate information about defects and failures **constructively**: stick to the facts,
> focus on the product rather than the person, and keep the shared goal of quality in view.

<SyllabusText>

Testers are often the bearers of bad news. It is a common human trait to blame the bearer of bad news. This makes communication skills crucial for testers.

Communicating test results may be perceived as criticism of the product and of its author.

Confirmation bias can make it difficult to accept information that disagrees with currently held beliefs.

Some people may perceive testing as a destructive activity, even though it contributes greatly to project success and product quality.

To try to improve this view, information about defects and failures should be communicated in a constructive way.

</SyllabusText>

### Whole Team Approach

<SyllabusRef ref="1.5.2" />

<Hub center="Everyone owns quality" caption="The whole-team approach, from Extreme Programming (CTFL 1.5.2)" items={['Tester', 'Developer', 'Product owner', 'Architect', 'Business analyst', 'Ops']} />

- **Any task** - Anyone with the right skills can do any task
- **Everyone** - is responsible for quality
- **Shared workspace** - physical or virtual

#### Testers collaborate with…

- **Business representatives** - to create suitable acceptance tests
- **Developers** - to agree the test strategy and test automation approach
- **The whole team** - to transfer testing knowledge and influence the product

- **Not always appropriate** - e.g. safety-critical systems may need a high level of test independence

<SyllabusText>

In the whole-team approach any team member with the necessary knowledge and skills can perform any task, and everyone is responsible for quality.

The team members share the same workspace (physical or virtual), as co-location facilitates communication and interaction.

Testers work closely with other team members to ensure that the desired quality levels are achieved. This includes collaborating with business representatives to help them create suitable acceptance tests and working with developers to agree on the test strategy and decide on test automation approaches.

Testers can thus transfer testing knowledge to other team members and influence the development of the product.

Depending on the context, the whole team approach may not always be appropriate. For instance, in some situations, such as safety-critical, a high level of test independence may be needed.

</SyllabusText>

<SyllabusRef ref="1.5.3" />

<IndependenceStairs />

### Independence: Pros and Cons

<SyllabusRef ref="1.5.3" />

- **Advantages** - Different cognitive biases from the author — so more effective at finding defects · Different backgrounds and perspectives spot different kinds of failures and defects · Can verify, challenge or disprove stakeholders' assumptions
- **Drawbacks** - Isolation from developers — less collaboration, communication problems, an adversarial relationship · Developers may lose a sense of responsibility for quality · Seen as a bottleneck, or blamed for release delays
- **Independence doesn't replace familiarity** - developers efficiently find many defects in their own code.

<SyllabusText>

A certain degree of independence makes the tester more effective at finding defects due to differences between the author's and the tester's cognitive biases (cf. Salman 1995). Independence is not, however, a replacement for familiarity, e.g., developers can efficiently find many defects in their own code.

The main benefit of independence of testing is that independent testers are likely to recognize different kinds of failures and defects compared to developers because of their different backgrounds, technical perspectives, and biases. Moreover, an independent tester can verify, challenge, or disprove assumptions made by stakeholders during specification and implementation of the system.

However, there are also some drawbacks. Independent testers may be isolated from the development team, which may lead to a lack of collaboration, communication problems, or an adversarial relationship with the development team. Developers may lose a sense of responsibility for quality. Independent testers may be seen as a bottleneck or be blamed for delays in release.

</SyllabusText>
